The first time I understood operations

At university, the clue came from an event.

It was a non-technical event at an engineering college, running on a fixed budget in a place where the technical events were supposed to draw the crowd. We negotiated what we could, visited nearby colleges to market it ourselves, and drew more participants than any other event. On the day, the problems arrived one after another, including things nobody had planned for. Solving them, keeping people moving and watching the whole thing come together was what made operations click for me.

That instinct found a harder environment at Vikram Engineering Works. Stainless steel, iron and CNC tooling had to arrive at the right price. Production had to keep moving. Customers in 11 states were waiting at the other end of the supply chain. A seasonal surface-roughness problem was hurting relationships with business customers, and the answer came through a technical fix rather than accepting complaints as part of manufacturing. Costs came down, daily output rose, and complaints fell sharply. The lesson stayed with me: a problem does not have to disappear completely before the work counts.

Then came Birmingham, and a different kind of pressure. When I joined Greggs, the store had no manager, training had stopped and daily sales had fallen by 25–30%. On my second day, a supervisor quit after a quarrel, leaving me on the front counter before I had been trained on the till or the products. The accent was difficult to understand. The pace was something I had never experienced in India. That night, I nearly called my manager to quit. Instead, the next shift became another problem to solve.

The difference came from looking closely at what the system was asking the team to do. We were producing more sandwiches than customers were buying, so production was reshaped around the real sales pattern. Savouries were baked closer to demand rather than left to go cold. Ordering changed with weekdays, weekends, weather, local events and bank holidays. Peak staffing was redesigned so the sandwich maker stayed where demand was highest. The store began to breathe again, with sales growing and waste falling.

Fresh Bake brought the same thinking into a new store, with a different obstacle: too many opinions and no clear chain of command. Being the youngest person at the table meant my ideas were often tried only after someone else's had failed. That was frustrating. So I kept building things that could be tested: layout, planograms, 5S, learning materials, routines, recruitment and supplier choices. A uniform quote that felt too high became a procurement exercise after hours, ending with a supplier switch and a much lower cost.

At Shake Shack, the problems arrived all at once. Opening the Birmingham site meant queues every day for 15 days, a largely new team, huge deliveries and the knowledge that a food-safety inspector could walk in at any time. Later, on International Burger Day, a major stock delivery arrived just as the doors opened. On another day, a stock count appeared to be thousands of pounds short until the previous count exposed the real issue. Those moments brought everything together: numbers, suppliers, people, compliance, customer experience and decisions made while the clock is running. The setting changes. The need to make the system work does not.

Case 1 · Stock control

The £4,000 shortage that wasn't

The stock count came back about £4,000 below what the system expected.

Most of it appeared to sit in five lines: chicken, chicken bites, beef, buns and fries. At first glance, that is the kind of number that demands an immediate explanation. A shortfall that large pulls everyone towards the latest figure, looking for something that must have gone wrong.

Then I went backwards. At the previous count, the stock system had been down. Those same five lines had shown surpluses of almost exactly the same size. The two counts told one story when viewed separately and another when put together.

So I netted them. Across both counts, the five lines came to under £40. The apparent £4,000 shortage was largely the system correcting the error from the previous count.

There was one exception: chicken bites. That line stayed short after the two counts were reconciled, so it was the one item I investigated.

What I took from itA variance should be traced through its history before it is treated as a loss. The number tells you where to look; the process tells you what happened.

Case 2 · International Burger Day

11:00, the doors open

At 10:30 on 28 May 2025, the queue was already forming behind the shutter.

International Burger Day meant buy-one-get-one-free on every burger. Before the doors opened, the shape of the day was already visible.

Then, at 11:00, the back door brought another problem: a delivery of about £4,000 of fresh and frozen stock. Everyone had over-ordered for what was expected to be a record day, and the delivery could not sit outside.

Two slower team members came off Expo to put it away. That left me alone on Expo with 15–20 orders on the screen while the queue kept moving.

The useful thing was what happened next. Most of the team knew most stations. When their own station came under control, people came over to help. The operation could move people towards the pressure point instead of leaving everyone fixed to their original position.

At 11:30 the dining room needed help, so I moved there. Once service was back on time, I stepped off the stations and watched the whole operation: restocking, compliance, temperature checks and guests. After 15:00, breaks were covered two at a time. The day finished at about £11,000 after discounts. There was no friction anywhere, and the team enjoyed the shift as much as I did.

What I took from itCross-training turns a staffing plan into a pool of options. The manager's job is to keep moving between the immediate problem and the whole operation.

Case 3 · Procurement & influence

The quote was too high

The uniform quote looked wrong.

At Fresh Bake, the first store was being built from scratch and every decision had another one waiting behind it. I was the youngest person at the table. About 90% of the time, other people tried their own ideas first. With many stakeholders and no clear chain of command, a decision agreed with my line manager could be overruled by someone else and later come back in a similar form.

After working hours, I researched local suppliers in Leicester, tested prices, and kept negotiating until one supplier agreed to my price. I told nobody until there was a significantly better quote to put in front of the Operations Director. He did his own due diligence and switched supplier.

Uniform costs came down by about 40%. Ingredients came down by about 5% using the same approach.

Being the youngest person in the room mattered less once there was something concrete to put on the table.
What I took from it: a tested solution gives an idea weight when authority is unclear. In procurement, the negotiation often starts with the work done before anyone enters the conversation.
Case 4 · People

The hire I nearly didn't make

He was new to the UK, still finding his words, and the interview did not go smoothly. My first instinct at Fresh Bake was to say no.

Then I remembered my own second day at Greggs: alone on a counter, unable to follow half of what customers said, and close to quitting that night. Someone had given me the chance to get through that week. So I hired him, and trained him on guest experience until the counter felt like his.

After I left Fresh Bake, he became the store's Assistant Manager. It is one of the things I am proudest of.

At Shake Shack, a new team member was so slow that I was close to ending his probation. After a shift I talked to him as a friend rather than a manager, and what he told me changed how I managed him. He showed me how to delegate to him, and two months later he was Employee of the Month. When I left, every member of the team wrote in my farewell card.

What I took from itI hire for energy and attitude, because everything else can be taught. And when someone is struggling, the conversation comes before the decision.
